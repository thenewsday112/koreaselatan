Tories warn Burnham’s EU ‘pandering’ will shred post-Brexit trade deals

📅 Oct 02, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

LONDON — The architects of Britain’s first post-Brexit trade deals have warned that the country’s independent trade policy is under threat after Andy Burnham pledged a major review of its relationship with the EU. 

Their intervention marks the latest salvo in a political fightback against Burnham’s EU policy as the Conservative Party conference gets underway in Birmingham this weekend.

Burnham this week vowed to discuss a revamped relationship with Europe, including rejoining the EU’s customs union, moving closer the bloc’s single market — or even pushing to outright rejoin the bloc. It marks a fundamental shift from a Labour Party that has previously ruled out all three.

Customs union membership — a popular option among some members of Burnham’s Cabinet — would ease trade between the U.K. and its largest trading partner, but require Britain to sacrifice the trade deals it has struck since leaving the bloc six years ago.

Greg Hands, who served as trade minister four times between 2016 to 2024 under Conservative governments, said that all options presented “grave consequences for Britain’s independent trade policy.”

“Depending exactly on what is agreed, it is likely to invalidate or make very difficult most — if not all — of those existing trade agreements, or many of those existing trade agreements,” he said. 

Since Brexit, the U.K. has negotiated free trade deals with nations including Australia, New Zealand, Japan and — more recently — the Asia Pacific trading bloc CPTPP, India, the Gulf states, as well as upgraded deals with South Korea and Switzerland.

Although the U.K. could technically copy-and paste EU versions of trade deals, Hands warned that the terms might not be so favorable, highlighting that the U.K.-Japan deal he had helped to negotiate “has got much more liberal rules on data” than the EU equivalent.

As a non-EU member, the U.K. would also surrender its ability to negotiate the terms of any new trade deals, Hands argued. “Why would the EU prioritize the U.K., which is not a paying member of the EU, over paying full members of the EU when it does trade agreements?”

But the issue goes further than free trade agreements, he said, pointing out that entering a customs union with the EU would affect the U.K.’s global tariff — the independent system of taxes and duty rates applied to goods imported from other countries. This would be a “huge diminution of the U.K.’s influence in the world to align or take the EU’s customs tariff,” Hands added.

Moreover, by signing up to a customs union, the U.K. would also lose its ability to erect its own trade defenses and be forced to implement EU measures like tariffs on steel and electric vehicles, Hands warned.

He also cautioned that closer alignment could complicate relations with the U.S., which has been critical of U.K. ambitions for closer alignment with the EU. “The U.S. is our single largest bilateral trading partner and I don’t think it would be in the U.K.’s interest to mess up that relationship.”

End of CPTPP membership

Liam Fox, a self-proclaimed “staunch euroskeptic” who served as trade secretary in the immediate aftermath of the referendum, between 2016 and 2019, said rejoining the EU or customs union would “mean an end to Britain’s independent trade policy and any strategy influence we have from membership of organizations such as [the Asia-Pacific trade bloc] CPTPP.” 

“This vague language from Andy Burnham is all about keeping the Labour left quiet whatever the consequences,” Fox added. “Rejoining the EU would mean being part of Schengen with free movement of people and adoption of the Euro. If that is what Andy Burnham wants, let him make the case for it, stop his mealy-mouthed pandering to his own party and start acting like a real prime minister.”

A third former Tory trade minister — granted anonymity to speak frankly — told MTUK the risks to Britain’s new trade deals was “real” and would “undermine new relationships with some of the fastest growing middle-class economies in the world.”

“As the mature EU markets continue to be a smaller part of U.K. export opportunities, the prime minister should be focusing on supporting U.K. businesses to win export contracts where huge new markets exist,” the former minister argued.

Pentagon excludes key allies from NATO meeting

📅 Oct 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Trump administration is excluding several major allies from a gathering of NATO members next month — after previous threats to deploy a “naughty and nice list” against governments that have run afoul of the president’s priorities.

The planned meeting in Poland, which Pentagon policy chief Elbridge Colby has spearheaded, is essentially a working group focused on NATO’s future, according to six diplomats familiar with the planning. But many of the countries who didn’t receive an invite — such as the United Kingdom, France and the Baltic countries — are big defense spenders and play key roles in the alliance, leaving their diplomats wondering why they’re being frozen out.

They question whether the administration is playing favorites in a way that could further splinter an alliance already struggling with increasing Russian aggression and depleted weapons stockpiles.

“We’re doing everything that is being asked of us,” said an official from an excluded ally nation. “We’re trying to understand what the criteria is and what the purpose of these meetings are. Not much has been passed on to us yet.”

The White House did not respond to a request for comment, and the Pentagon declined to comment.

President Donald Trump and his top lieutenants have relentlessly criticized NATO since returning to power, on everything from members’ refusal to back the Iran war to the failure of some countries to spend 5% of their GDP on defense.

“I always considered NATO, where we spend Hundreds of Billions of Dollars per year protecting these same Countries, to be a one way street — We will protect them, but they will do nothing for us, in particular, in a time of need,” Trump railed in a Truth Social post this year. Last month, he snubbed another ally — South Korea — by abruptly scaling back a planned joint military exercise while complaining about Seoul’s refusal to join the war against Iran.

In April, as POLITICO first reported, the White House came up with something akin to a who’s-in-and-who’s-out list while it looked for ways to punish allies who didn’t adhere to Trump’s wishes.

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth has echoed that vision. He has encouraged “model allies” and warned that those that do not support U.S. interests will suffer consequences.

While details on the new NATO working group are still vague, it follows the threat of rewarding some at the expense of others.

Colby has held two similar meetings with invitees Norway, Sweden, Finland, Poland, Germany and the Netherlands. The countries involved in the meeting — known as the Bergen Group format — insist it’s not meant to rupture the alliance, said a diplomat from one of the invited nations, who like others, was granted anonymity to discuss internal deliberations. Instead, the group intends to come up with ideas it can bring to the full alliance for more stress-testing.

“This can be an engine for transformation,” for the whole alliance, the diplomat said. The person acknowledged that some allies are frustrated by the exclusion, especially since many have hit or surpassed the spending targets demanded by the Trump administration.

The U.S. might add Denmark to the exclusive club next given the agreement reached between the two countries to expand the American military footprint in Greenland, according to another person familiar with the planning.

Colby, who has been the catalyst for the initiative, held a first meeting of its kind in Bergen, Norway, in June and invited the six favored allies. “NATO allies that are rapidly meeting their Hague spending commitments, have the scale to contribute, and are ready to get down to practical business,” he wrote on social media after the meeting.

He noted this month at a conference in Washington that “the idea is to pull the whole alliance along,” adding that it “will be a better and stronger NATO that is more durable” and give Europe more military capacity.

Colby has taken a longtime interest in smaller format talks between NATO nations. He has focused in previous years on reviving Germany as a military power to contain Russia and dedicating American troops to deal with China’s rise.

But even invited allies question why Colby and other defense officials have cherry-picked attendees and left out some of the continent’s largest defense powers, such as Britain.

“If they try splitting NATO into the good ones and the bad ones, it is dangerous for the U.S.,” said another of the six diplomats. “But for Europe, this is existential.”

Britain needs tougher steel trade defenses, Cabinet minister says

📅 Sep 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

LIVERPOOL, England — Wales Secretary Stephen Kinnock is pushing for tougher steel trade defenses for British producers, amid concerns firms are being undercut by cheap imports.

Speaking at The POLITICO Pub at Labour Party conference on Monday, Kinnock said he is in discussions with Trade Minister Anas Sarwar about strengthening protections for the steel industry, arguing that the government needs to act to ensure domestic producers are competing on a level playing field.

His comments came after Tata Steel’s Llanwern plant in Newport, South Wales, saw its production capacity slashed to around 50% as it struggles to compete against a glut of cheaper, foreign galvanized steel imports.

Asked whether he is satisfied with the current position on steel tariffs and quotas, and whether the government should reconsider its approach before the measures are reviewed next July, Kinnock said: “I am not at all happy with the position on quotas and tariffs. There is clear evidence that the British steel industry is being undercut.”

In July this year, under Keir Starmer’s administration, the U.K. implemented a new steel trade measure, replacing the expiring EU-era safeguard regime with a 51% reduction in overall tariff-free quota volumes compared to previous levels.

But Kinnock complained that some of the original measures were “diluted,” resulting in plants like Llanwern being undercut by imports of galvanized steel from places like Vietnam, Turkey, and South Korea.

“We absolutely have to do something about that,” he said. “That is one of my top priorities as the Secretary of State for Wales.”

Kinnock added that he is liaising closely with Sarwar on the issue, adding that an independent auditor is also assessing the damage caused by potential dumping.

“I’m confident that we will get a result from that, which will enable us to have a more robust trade defense regime,” he said.

Asked about a timeline for the investigation, he said: “We want it to be very rapid, and the trade remedies agency is is ready to act. It just needs the data. The data is being collected now. I am pushing as hard as I possibly can to have some movement on this before Christmas.”

Responding to reports from Sky News that the Indian owner of Port Talbot has approached Whitehall for an additional multi-million support package amid delays to its new electric arc furnace, Kinnock confirmed that he would give “careful consideration to any proposal that they might have for financial support.”

The sum would be in addition to the £500 million government grant awarded to Tata Steel in 2023 to build the furnace.
